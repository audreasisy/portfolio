1The problem

Recruitment ran through one person wearing the HR/BizOps hat — me — across a scatter of Google tools and Slack. The hiring itself (screening, interviews, judgment) was fine being human. The problem was all the manual bridging work between tools that surrounded it:

  • every candidate meant downloading a CV from email, filing it into the right Drive folder, and typing them into a tracker by hand;
  • every interview stage meant writing an individual invitation or rejection email, pasting in the correct booking link, and pinging the panel;
  • every offer meant hand-assembling an offer letter from a previous candidate's document — re-typing the salary and probation math, with the arithmetic risk that carries — then a probation contract and an NDA, re-keying the same personal details again.

Each step was repetitive, error-prone (wrong folder, wrong figure, a duplicated or forgotten email), and it simply didn't scale when several roles were open at once. There was also no single place to see where every role stood.

2What I built

HireDesk — an end-to-end recruitment automation system that mirrors the company's real hiring funnel and removes the mechanical work at every stage, while leaving all human judgment with people. It runs from a custom 💼 HireDesk menu built into the spreadsheets, with no paid software, no external hiring platform, and no third-party add-ons — only the Google Workspace + Slack the company already used. On the relevant click, the system:

  1. Provisions a new opening — creates the role's Drive folder tree, copies a fresh tracker from a template, writes the role's configuration into it, files the JD, records the requisition, and can notify the team on Slack.
  2. Pulls CVs in automatically — reads the intake inboxes, saves each attachment to the right folder, and appends the candidate as a new tracker row (the tracker being the single source of truth).
  3. Drafts the right email at every stage — marking a candidate Pass or Fail drafts the correct message (R1 invitation, R2 invitation, or the matching rejection), bilingual, with the booking link and location built in.
  4. Generates the offer — one click produces the offer letter as a Google Doc with all salary and probation math done automatically, then drafts the offer email, choosing the wording variant by the candidate's level.
  5. Generates the hire paperwork — one click produces the bilingual probation contract and the NDA together, auto-filling the new hire's details from the employee sheet, and logs the contract in a register.
  6. Keeps a live company-wide pipeline — every candidate rolls up from their role's tracker into a master sheet, so the whole hiring picture sits in one place.

The architecture — two stores, joined by one ID

The system is deliberately split into two file types joined by a shared Req ID, each with its own bound Apps Script project. The design point: because all the candidate-email, offer, and contract logic lives in one Tracker Template, every new role inherits the full automation just by being a copy of it.

3Key design decisions

  • A two-store architecture that scales itself. All inheritable logic lives in one Tracker Template, so opening a new role costs a single kickoff click — every tracker gets the full automation for free.
  • Decision-driven email engine. An installable trigger watches the decision columns and drafts the right email per stage, with the wording fully separated from the logic — copy can change without touching the code.
  • Drafts, never sends — by design. Automation does the assembly; a person keeps the final say. Templates omit a signature so Gmail appends the operator's own.
  • Guardrails against real-world mess. Only the exact values Pass/Fail fire anything; a row missing a name or email writes a "needs details" note instead of a broken draft; and a dedupe engine stamps each stage so nothing double-drafts.
  • Branching that matches the real process. One-round roles skip R2 automatically; onsite invites show a Maps-linked address and parking note while online ones show a meeting note; and the offer email auto-selects a Trainee vs. Junior-Executive variant by level.
  • Derived math, never hand-typed. Offers and contracts take only basic salary and lunch; probation salary, totals, and the probation end date all derive automatically.
  • Resilient, accent-aware documents. The contract module matches a hire against the employee sheet accent- and case-insensitively, falls back to a blank form if they aren't listed yet, and generates the contract and NDA independently so one failing can't lose the other.

5What I owned end-to-end

I scoped the system by mapping the company's real recruitment workflow and deciding what to automate and what to leave human — no online forms, screening and interviews stay with people, only tools the company already paid for. I designed the architecture (the two-store model joined by Req ID, with all inheritable logic in one template) and built all the automation — provisioning, CV intake, the stage-driven email engine, the offer module with its automatic math and variant selection, and the one-click contract-plus-NDA generator.

Along the way I diagnosed and fixed subtle failures — most notably a stale-folder-ID bug in the shared-template design that was silently routing generated documents to the wrong place — and I codified a set of hard-won engineering rules (locale handling that otherwise breaks every generated formula, trigger-safe UI patterns, and self-contained dialogs that survive a non-standard browser) so the system stays stable in production. Finally, I documented and handed it off through a stage-by-stage series of handovers and an end-user guide.

The build used an AI-assisted, pair-programming workflow — I directed an AI coding assistant to my design and spec, reviewed and corrected its output, and drove the testing. The architecture, domain logic, corrections, and decisions are mine; the AI was an accelerator working under my direction.
